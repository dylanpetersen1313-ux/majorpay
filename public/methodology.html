Methodology

Last updated September 26, 2026.

Every number on this site comes from two federal datasets, processed the same way for every school and major. This page says exactly which files, which years, and what we do when data is missing.

That's the complete list. We don't mix in other salary sources such as schools' own career-outcome reports, Bureau of Labor Statistics occupation data, or web searches. Those describe different people, measured at different times, in different ways, so they can't be compared with each other or with the federal figures.

Earnings

The salary shown is the Scorecard field EARN_MDN_4YR: median annual earnings in the fourth full year after completing the degree. For this release that means students who finished in the 2017-18 and 2018-19 award years, with earnings measured in calendar years 2022 and 2023, adjusted for inflation to 2024 dollars.

Earnings come from IRS tax records (W-2 wages plus self-employment income), matched by the Department of Education. A graduate is only counted if they received federal financial aid, were working, and were not enrolled in school during the year earnings were measured.

Every salary on the site uses this same 4-year measure. Earlier versions fell back to the 1-year figure when the 4-year one was missing, which put first-year and fourth-year pay in the same comparison. That fallback is gone.

Cost

Prices are for the 2026-27 academic year. The most recent years are projected forward from each school's own historical IPEDS growth rate, so treat them as estimates.

  • No income selected: the sticker price for the residency you pick (tuition, fees, room and board as the school reports it). With no residency picked, the school's average net price across all aided students.
  • Income selected: the school's average net price for families in that income bracket, meaning what they paid after grants and scholarships. These figures describe in-state students. At a public school, choosing out-of-state adds the out-of-state premium (out-of-state sticker minus in-state sticker), since need-based aid rarely covers it. Private schools charge everyone the same.
  • Four-year cost is the annual cost times four. No tuition increases are added during those four years.

The payoff column

"Salary minus yearly cost" is the year-4 salary minus one quarter of the four-year cost. It's a single-year snapshot meant for ranking options against each other. No discounting is applied, and it ignores loan interest, taxes, raises after year 4, and the earnings you give up while in school. It is not a lifetime return.

Missing and suppressed data

  • The Department of Education withholds earnings (marked PS, privacy suppressed) when a program has too few graduates in the data. We treat those as missing. We never estimate or fill them in.
  • Programs with no published 4-year figure don't appear in the major list. In this release that left 27,048 bachelor's programs at 1,917 schools.
  • Schools with no price data still show salaries, with cost marked as unavailable.

Limitations

Selection, not cause. Earnings differences between schools and majors partly reflect who chooses them: prior preparation, family resources, location, career goals. A school with high median pay didn't necessarily cause it. The numbers describe past graduates, not what attending will do for a particular person.

Grad school leaves the data. Anyone enrolled in school during the measurement year is excluded. Majors where many graduates go on to medical school, law school or PhDs (biology, chemistry, psychology, pre-law tracks) are represented mostly by those who didn't, which can make them look worse or better than they really are.

Federal aid recipients only. Students who never received federal aid aren't in the earnings data. At schools where most students pay full price without federal aid, the median reflects a smaller, different slice of graduates.

Four years is early. Pay in some fields grows slowly and then fast. A year-4 snapshot can understate careers like medicine, law or academia and overstate fields that plateau early.

Why not just Google the price data?

Search a school's cost and you usually get its sticker price, from its own site or a ranking page, often for a different year than the next school you look up. Sticker price is what almost nobody on aid pays. What a family actually pays depends on income, and that net price is rarely easy to find school by school.

Search "average salary for [major] at [school]" and you get numbers from self-reported salary sites, school marketing pages, and articles, each measured differently: different years after graduation, different definitions of "graduate," often no sample size at all. Two of those numbers can't be fairly compared.

Here, every salary is the same federal tax-record measure, four years out, for every school and major. Every cost is the same IPEDS price data, and when you give an income it's the net price families in that bracket actually paid, not the sticker price. The math is identical for every result, so a comparison between two schools is a comparison of like with like.
